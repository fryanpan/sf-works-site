  RE: Response to the 2025-2026 San Francisco Civil Grand Jury Report:
      "When Making Do Doesn't Work: San Francisco Jails in Crisis"
  Dear Presiding Judge East:
  Pursuant to Penal Code section 933.05, the San Francisco District Attorney respectfully
  submits the following response to the findings and recommendations directed to the
  Office in the 2025-2026 San Francisco Civil Grand Jury Report, "When Making Do
  Doesn't Work: San Francisco Jails in Crisis."

  FINDING F1
  "The City and County leadership would benefit from first-hand knowledge obtained
  through inspection of the jail facilities and operations to enable them to better understand
  how policy changes affecting budget and operations for the Jails impact the operation of
  the Jails."
  Response to F1:
  The District Attorney partially disagrees with Finding F1.
  The District Attorney agrees that familiarity with the operation of the county jail system
  is beneficial to public officials who have authority to direct funding to the Jails or who
  otherwise have oversight of the Sheriff’s Department.
  While the District Attorney recognizes the importance of understanding the conditions
  under which incarcerated persons and Sheriff's personnel operate, the finding does not
  properly apply to the District Attorney because it overstates the relationship between jail
  inspections and the ability of the District Attorney to address the challenges identified
  throughout the report. The District Attorney does not control jail facilities, jail staffing,
  jail operations, inmate classification, facility maintenance, transportation systems,
  treatment capacity, capital planning, or funding decisions. Nor does the District Attorney
  control judicial detention decisions, court calendars, trial scheduling, or sentencing.

  Accordingly, while site visits may provide additional context, they do not expand the
  District Attorney's authority to address the infrastructure, staffing, operational, and
  funding challenges identified in the report.

  RECOMMENDATION R1-A(iv)
  "The District Attorney should inspect the San Francisco and San Bruno Jails by March 1,
  2027, and at least once every two years thereafter."
  Response to R1-A(iv):
  The recommendation will not be implemented because it is not warranted.
  While the District Attorney may elect to participate in future jail tours or inspections as
  circumstances warrant, the report does not establish that a mandatory recurring inspection
  by the District Attorney would address the principal concerns identified in the report or
  could be addressed through the District Attorney's statutory duties and authority.
  The report identifies deficiencies relating primarily to infrastructure, capital planning,
  staffing, technology systems, facility maintenance, transportation resources, and
  treatment capacity. These are matters outside the authority of the District Attorney's
  Office and the report does not explain how recurring inspections by the District Attorney
  would correct the principal causes of the current challenges. Accordingly, a mandatory
  inspection schedule is not warranted as a corrective measure.

  FINDING F3
  "The Jails are dangerously close to capacity, and SFSO has not convened the Crowding
  Committee despite meeting the capacity triggers outlined in the SFSO Custody and Court
  Operations Manual, Policy 511.2."
  Response to F3:
  The District Attorney partially disagrees with Finding F3.
  The District Attorney agrees that the Sheriff did not convene a Crowding Committee.
  However, it is unclear whether the factual and policy prerequisites for convening such a
  committee were met, either at the time of the report’s publication or presently. Sheriff's
  Policy 511.2 provides that "[i]n the event that the incarcerated person population remains
  over capacity or continues to increase, a crowding committee will be formed at the
  direction of the Sheriff . . . .". Whether those conditions have been satisfied is a
  determination committed in the first instance to the Sheriff's Department.
  Further, publicly available Sheriff's Department data suggests that current population
  levels may differ materially from those discussed in the report. As of June 9, 2026, the
  reported jail population was 1,157, with a 30-day average daily population of 1,177 and a
  median population of 1,173. Those figures are substantially below the levels cited in the
  report and represent less than 80 percent of the rated jail capacity. Accordingly, the
  District Attorney lacks sufficient information to conclude that the conditions
  contemplated by Policy 511.2 have been satisfied.

  Additionally, it is unclear that convening a Crowding Committee would materially
  address the underlying causes identified in the report. The report recognizes that the
  current jail population largely reflects individuals charged with felony offenses, including
  a substantial number charged with serious or violent crimes, and that misdemeanor-only
  arrestees generally do not become part of the long-term housed population. The report
  also notes that current jail populations are simply approaching levels previously
  experienced by the system prior to the extraordinary reductions associated with the
  COVID-19 pandemic.
  To the extent the finding suggests that jail capacity concerns can be materially addressed
  through actions within the authority of the District Attorney's Office, the District
  Attorney disagrees. The District Attorney does not determine jail capacity, control
  facility operations, manage detention resources, or possess unilateral authority to release
  incarcerated individuals whose continued detention has been ordered by the court
  pursuant to statutory and constitutional standards designed to protect public safety.
  While the report expresses concern regarding available capacity, tellingly it does not
  identify specific categories of incarcerated individuals who could be safely released in a
  manner that would materially reduce population pressures while remaining consistent
  with public-safety considerations, statutory requirements, and judicial detention orders.
  Indeed, because detention decisions are ultimately made by the courts under
  constitutional and statutory standards that include public-safety considerations, many of
  the individuals contributing to the housed population are already subject to judicial
  determinations that continued detention is warranted.
  While the District Attorney agrees that jail population trends and available capacity
  warrant continued attention, the report itself identifies a complex set of contributing
  factors—none of which is controlled by the District Attorney. To the extent the report
  suggests that jail population reductions are readily achievable through actions of criminal
  justice stakeholders after a convening of the Crowding Committee, the District Attorney
  respectfully disagrees.

  RECOMMENDATION R3-A
  "SFSO should convene the Crowding Committee. In addition to the persons specified in
  Policy 511.2(a)-(g), representatives of the District Attorney, San Francisco Sheriff's
  Department Oversight Board, and the Board of Supervisors should also be invited."
  Response to R3-A:
  The recommendation is directed primarily at the Sheriff's Department, however, given
  current data the recommendation does not appear warranted.
  If the recommendation is implemented, and to the extent that it contemplates
  participation by the District Attorney's Office, the District Attorney may elect to
  participate in discussions concerning jail population trends and criminal justice system
  operations if invited. However, such participation by the District Attorney would not
  materially expand the authority of the District Attorney's Office to address the primary
  infrastructure, staffing, and funding challenges identified in the report because the
  District Attorney’s role within the criminal justice system is simply to institute criminal
  proceedings for public offenses when supported by the evidence and the law.

  The District Attorney notes that the report itself identifies numerous structural causes of
  the current challenges, including historical facility decisions, deferred capital
  investments, aging facilities, staffing shortages, treatment resource limitations,
  operational demands, and funding constraints. While participation in a Crowding
  Committee may facilitate communication among stakeholders, the report identifies few
  issues that fall within the authority of the District Attorney to remedy.
  The report assumes that meaningful reductions in the jail population are both available
  and advisable, yet it acknowledges that the overwhelming majority of housed inmates are
  charged with felony offenses and that more than half are charged with serious or violent
  crimes. The report does not identify any specific population that could be safely released
  in sufficient numbers to materially reduce crowding while remaining consistent with the
  public-safety considerations, risk assessments, statutory requirements, and judicial
  findings that govern detention decisions. At the same time, the report expresses concern
  that some individuals are released from custody too quickly to receive meaningful
  treatment or stabilization services. These observations appear difficult to reconcile and, in
  the District Attorney's view, demonstrate that the challenges identified in the report are
  more complex than can be addressed through generalized population-reduction measures
  alone. Absent the identification of a specific, lawful, and publicly safe means of
  materially reducing the jail population, it is also unclear what actions a Crowding
  Committee could recommend that would address the root causes identified by the report.
  The District Attorney remains willing to engage in good-faith discussions with criminal
  justice partners regarding public safety, lawful detention practices, and the efficient
  operation of the criminal justice system. However, the Office will continue to advocate
  for detention where warranted by law and public-safety considerations and will not alter
  those assessments, or advocate for the release of otherwise lawfully detained individuals,
  solely to address longstanding infrastructure, staffing, or funding deficiencies within the
  jail system. Meaningful resolution of the challenges identified in the report will
  ultimately require action by those entities with responsibility for jail operations, facilities,
  treatment resources, staffing, and funding, rather than adjustments to detention positions
  that are otherwise supported by law and public-safety considerations.
  Respectfully submitted,



  Ana Gonzalez
  Chief Assistant District Attorney
  San Francisco District Attorney’s Office

DISCHARGE PLANNING Staff work between 2pm and 10pm Monday through Friday, providing linkage services to high need and vulnerable persons.

Safe Passage The objective of the Safe Passage program is to provide transportation to persons released from the
SF County jails at night who have requested assistance; or when staff have identified a transportation need.

Safe Harbor Operated by Discharge Planning staff, this program seeks to immediately address emergency housing
needs of vulnerable clients following release from jail by providing a safe room until service providers are available.

MEDI-CAL ENROLLMENT ASSISTANCE and CalAIM The California Advancing and Innovating Medi-Cal (CalAIM) is a multi-year reform initiative led by the
CA Department of Health Care Services to improve health equity and includes a focus on the justice impacted population and a mandate that everyone booked into a
correctional facility be screened for insurance status and be provided with enrollment assistance if eligible.

HOUSING ACCESS POINT A DHSH contracted provider conducts assessments to determine whether one qualifies for
Permanent Supportive Housing (PSH) opportunities and/or additional financial support services for adults experiencing homelessness.

MEDICATION ASSISTED TREATMENT LINKAGE Individuals who have initiated medication assisted treatment for
opioid or alcohol use disorders while in jail are connected by Jail Health Services
to community providers for on-going treatment and provision of medication.

            1.0 Introduction

            1.1         Purpose
            This Project Management Plan documents the structure, governance, and roadmap for
            Harris Corrections (HCOR) delivery approach for the Offender360™ (O360) solution for
            San Francisco Sheriff’s Office (SFSO). The plan serves as the source of truth for the
            details of the engagement and the activities throughout its lifecycle. It is encouraged
            that all project team members review the plan regularly and as needed.

            1.1.1        Responsibility for the Project Management Plan
            The HCOR and SFSO Project Managers are responsible for ensuring updates are
            captured in the project management plan as required throughout the project, and that
            the updates are socialized appropriately. All changes to the document will be captured
            in the Document Version History.
            The Project Sponsor(s) are responsible for approving the original document and any
            updated formal versions that follow.



            1.2         Background
            The primary objective of this project is to replace SFSO’s current (legacy) Jail
            Management System (JMS) with Offender360™, a feature-rich, modern, cloud-based
            JMS and for HCOR to provide support of Offender360™ after go-live. Additionally, to
            improve critical data sharing between SFSO partners; meet California Department of
            Justice data compliance and protection standards; and implement strong security to
            protect critical information and infrastructure according to the California Law
            Enforcement Telecommunications System.



            1.3         Project Vision and Goals
            The primary goals of this project are to:
                 •    implement the Offender360™ Jail Management System, meeting the
                      requirements identified in phase 1 and confirmed in phase 2;
                 •    implement the in-scope data interfaces;
                 •    migrate applicable data from the legacy JMS, SWAP, and Sentinel systems to
                      Offender360™;
                 •    provide train-the-trainer end-user and system administration training; and
                 •    improve Data and Infrastructure Security in accordance with California DOJ
                      CLETS and CJIS mandates

            5.3           Risk and Issue Management

            5.3.1         Risk Management
            Risk Management involves identifying, assessing, mitigating, and managing a project’s
            risks. The Risk Management Process will enable the project team to create mitigations
            that effectively address potential barriers to success. The Risk Management Framework
            provides the process for identifying and reporting risks. It also provides the structure for
            how risks will be weighted/scored and how the impact and category of impact are
            identified. When a risk is presented, according to the Risk Management Framework, it
            will require validation from the project team and then be assigned to a team member to
            develop a mitigation plan.

            5.3.2         Definition of Risks
            A risk is defined as any concern that could impact the ability of the project to meet its
            schedule, cost, or other outcomes. A risk can arise from either external or, more often,
            from internal factors such as invalid assumptions, limited access to resources, or overly
            optimistic plans. Risks are measured in terms of severity as determined by probability of
            occurrence and impact to the project. Risks have two components:
                      •    The probability of the risk occurring
                      •    The expected impact of the risk if it occurs
            Unlike issues, risks relate to events that could occur and may affect the project’s
            outcomes. An issue is a risk that has occurred.

            5.3.3         Roles and Responsibilities
            The key players include:
            Risk Owner: The Risk Owner is any person on the project who identifies a risk.
            Risk Assignee: The Risk Assignee is the person responsible for developing mitigation
            options.
            HCOR Project Manager
            The HCOR Project Manager will be responsible for entering and updating data into the
            Risk Log, as well as doing regular maintenance to the Risk Log.
            The HCOR Project Manager will validate and address potential risks identified, report,
            and communicate these risks to the weekly project status meeting for discussion and
            mitigation (if possible). Some risks may require escalation to the Project Steering
            Committee.
            Project Steering Committee
            Address risks escalated by the core team from the weekly meetings.

            5.4         Issue Management

            5.4.1        Introduction
            Issue management is the process for identification, analysis, resolution, reporting, and
            escalation of a project’s issues. The process will enable the project team to create
            strategies to effectively address potential barriers to success.

            5.4.2        Definition of Issues
            While risks emphasize planning and mitigating, issues are action-oriented to be
            addressed or resolved ad hoc. Issues require resolution. The Issues Management
            process is not meant to capture every question that arises during the project, every to-
            do, or every discussion within the team but rather to capture those issues which are
            actively affecting the project in some respect and require resolution.
            Issues should be:
                      • Resolvable with action items
                      • Proactively discovered during the project
            Issues should NOT be:
                      • Questions/suggestions for other team members that might be resolved quickly
                      • Tasks that are already listed in the Work Business Structure (WBS) and
                      scheduled to be completed
                      • Anything that should be documented elsewhere (e.g., meeting minutes, design
                      documents, task lists)

            5.4.3        Issue Management Approach
            The Issue Management approach provides a clear understanding to the project team for
            how to manage and process issues as they arise. The Issue Management approach
            and process is put in place to document, track progress, and resolve issues with
            minimal impact on projects. Furthermore, understanding that risks will, or may become
            an issue once they are realized, the Issue Management approach should be viewed
            independently for unforeseen occurrences and barriers, but also used as the escalation
            path for a realized risk. In other words, the same Issue Management process will be
            used for managing both issues arising from identified risks as well as those that emerge
            unexpectedly. This ensures that the management of issues and the treatment of risks
            are considered in an appropriately integrated manner.
            The Issue Management approach is a methodical process whereby the project team will
            identify, clarify and validate, categorize, score, and prioritize all issues based on
            realized impact or possible impact. The project team may call on various team members
            or vested stakeholders to help identify issues or bring them to their attention according
            to the defined process.

            9.0 Training Plan
            Training is a key contributor to user adoption and change management activities. It is
            important that users feel prepared and ready to use the system in advance of go-live to
            realize the benefits that the new system is expected to provide.

            HCOR’s approach to training starts with incremental exposure to the system through
            engagement with end users throughout the project. This begins with participation in the
            discovery process to provide input into the system configuration needs to align with the
            SFSO requirements. End user involvement continues through the configuration phase
            to provide feedback on the proposed system changes and iteration demonstrations.
            End Users will be heavily involved in User Acceptance testing and will ultimately make
            the system acceptance decision at the conclusion of UAT.

            More formalized training for SFSO end-users will be provided as part of Train-the-
            Trainer Training and SFSO System Administrators will be provided with training on how
            to support the solution. The details and schedule for both Train-the-Trainer Training and
            System Administration training will be fully documented in the Training Plan

            11.0 Liquidated Damages
            Liquidated damages are fully defined in amendment one of the contract, article 2,
            section 4.6.

            11.1 Overview
            The agreement includes provisions for liquidated damages should the project be
            delayed. HCOR is responsible for payment of $185,000.00 to SFSO if the go-live date is
            after the liquidated damages date; however, if the delays are wholly or partially due to
            SFSO then a new date for the liquidated damages needs to be established.

            11.2 Identification of Delays
            The HCOR Project Manager will notify the SFSO Project Manager when delays which
            SFSO is either wholly or partially responsible for and if agreed by SFSO a revised date
            for the liquidated damages will be established.

            11.3 Process
            The HCOR Project Manager shall be responsible for identifying a project delay which is
            wholly or partially caused by SFSO. These delays shall be documented in a Liquidated
            Damages Adjustment Form like the one depicted in Figure 4. The document shall be
            submitted to the SFSO Project Manager for review. Once the HCOR and SFSO project
            managers have come to an agreement, the document shall be submitted for signature
            by the respective designated signatories:
                      •    Martin Okumu, CIO San Francisco Sheriff’s Office
                      •    Carrie Young, VP Harris Corrections

            11.4 Tracking
            The HCOR Project Manager shall log each form with the date it was logged, a brief
            description of the reason for the delay, the length of the delay, the date the form was
            approved, and the new liquidated damages date.
            It should be noted that there may be more than one Liquidated Damages Adjustment
            Form in-flight at any one time.

            12.0 Project Change Control
            This process is referenced in the contract amendment 1 Appendix C-1 Part 2.

            12.1 Change Control Process

            12.1.1 Submitting a Change Request
            Either Party may submit a written request for a Contract Change to the other Party
            (Change Request). The receiving Party will evaluate the Change Request and deliver to
            the requesting Party a written response within fifteen (15) Business Days following
            receipt of the Change Request. In respect of SFSO requested changes, HCOR’s
            response may include associated proposed operational, financial, and other
            modifications to the SOW and Services that HCOR believes reasonable to implement
            this Agreement Change (Change Proposal).
            Neither a Change Request nor a Change Proposal have a mandated format, except that
            Change Requests and Change Proposals must be reasonably sufficient for the
            receiving Party to understand the request or proposal (as applicable).

            12.1.2 Replying to a Change Request
            To the extent the Parties agree in writing to implement a Contract Change, the Parties
            will promptly implement, and document matters related to this Agreement Change to the
            extent reasonably necessary. For example, a minor operational Contract Change will
            most likely not require significant documentation beyond the exchanges regarding this
            Agreement Change, while more significant Contract Changes may require an
            amendment to the applicable Contract.
            To the extent that a Party makes an agreement to a Contract change conditional (where
            HCOR provides SFSO with a Change Proposal), the other Party will decide in its sole
            discretion to either accept, reject or negotiate such conditions. Neither Party will be
            required to implement a Contract Change until and unless any applicable conditions are
            agreed upon, and either Party may cease negotiations at any time and for any reason.

            12.1.3 Implementing a Change Request
            To the extent a Contract Change’s conditions are successfully negotiated, the Parties
            must promptly implement this Agreement Change as agreed, and document matters to
            the extent reasonably necessary. Until such time as the Agreement Change is accepted
            by either party, HCOR shall continue to supply the Services as originally agreed.
            Budget and/or schedule increases or decreases incurred from the following are subject
            to a Change Request in the event of:
                      •    Additional requirements and scope not originally included in scope of the
                           SOW that are subsequently included in scope
                      •    Delays and rework caused by SFSO or HCOR related to a request for
                           material change in the Scope of Work

Pursuant to its regulatory and oversight authority, the Sheriff's Department Oversight Board (SDOB) has
formally reviewed, debated, and approved individual responses to the Civil Grand Jury’s findings and
recommendations. In the interest of clarity, transparency, and public accountability, the Board has

access mandates into this unified transmittal.
Civil Grand Jury Finding F1
City and County leadership would benefit from first-hand knowledge obtained through inspection of the
jail facilities and operations to enable them to better understand how policy changes affecting budget and
operations for the Jails impact the operation of the Jails.
Sheriff's Department Oversight Board (SDOB) Response to Finding Number 1 (F1)
The Sheriff's Department Oversight Board agrees with Finding Number F1 and submits the following
written response:
The Sheriff's Department Oversight Board (SDOB) will resume monthly jail visits conducted in
compliance with the Brown Act and applicable confidentiality requirements. Visits will include both
scheduled visits and, where permitted, unannounced or short-notice visits to observe actual operating
conditions. The Board recommends that the Sheriff designate a standing liaison to facilitate access.
Visits will examine movement and cell-time programming, health services, visitation, classification,
facility conditions, library access, and lockdowns. Visits will also include the Reset Center, hospital
settings serving people in custody (including the San Francisco General Psychiatric Ward), and the
SDOB Response to Civil Grand Jury September 28, 2026
psychiatric unit at County Jail #2 (CJ2), subject to applicable access and privacy requirements. When
possible, Board members will hear directly from front-line employees and people in custody.
Visits will include follow-ups on complaints and additional inspections when extraordinary public
concerns arise. The Board considers an Inspector General essential
to sustain independent jail oversight and coordination of inspections to address and follow through on this
recommendation.
The Board also recommends visits no less than quarterly by elected officials and representatives of
coordinating departments and agencies. This includes, but is not limited to, representatives of the Mayor's
Office, the Board of Supervisors, the Department of Public Health, the District Attorney’s Office, the
Public Defender’s Office, Prisoner Legal Services, and other relevant agencies. These visits should
provide firsthand knowledge to inform decisions concerning jail funding, conditions, and policies.
Participants should receive an orientation or written briefing addressing jail operations, staffing, budget
constraints, and unresolved oversight concerns, The Board also requests structured debriefings on
inspections and investigations that protect confidential information while identifying operational risks,
budget consequences, staffing deficiencies, and impacts on people in custody, employees, families, and
the public.
Grand Jury Finding F3
The Jails are dangerously close to capacity, and SFSO has not convened the Crowding Committee despite
meeting the capacity triggers outlined in the SFSO Custody and Court Operations Manual, Policy 511.2.
SDOB Response to Finding Number Three (F3)
The Sheriff's Department Oversight Board (SDOB) shares the Civil Grand Jury’s concern regarding jail
crowding in Finding F3 and submits the following written response:
The SDOB emphasizes from the outset that jail overcrowding and staffing strain are systemic, system-
caused issues driven by outside entities—including the Judiciary, judges determining bail and sentencing,
the District Attorney’s Office, and law enforcement operations (such as the Dimock operation).
Consequently, this is not a burden that should be shouldered alone by the Sheriff's Office.
Furthermore, the SDOB strongly believes that jails should not serve as holding facilities or mental health
hospitals for individuals suffering from dual diagnoses, acute mental health struggles, or substance abuse.
To address these compounding issues, jail capacity must be assessed by more than a physica! bed count.
The assessment must account for classification, medical and behavioral health needs, security
requirements, unusable housing units, staffing levels, and a heavy reliance on mandated overtime. It must
evaluate how staffing strategies and forced overtime affect employee fatigue, call-outs, safe operations,
and the overall care and safety of people in custody.
To ensure transparent operational oversight, the Board requires the following separated reporting tracks:

SDOB Response to Civil Grand Jury September 28, 2026
disaggregated population and operational data quarterly to the Sheriff, Board of Supervisors,
SDOB, and the public (subject to confidentiality requirements). Reporting must distinguish
between people awaiting processing, short-term detention, and those requiring housing
assignments. It must track parole-related detention, length of stay, unavailable housing units,
staffing shortages, mandatory overtime, and service interruptions. SFSO must explain how these
factors relate to the convening triggers in Policy 511.2. Reliable data collection and timely
analysis must be supported by adequate funding and technology, with assistance from the Office
of the Inspector General.

e Crowding Committee Convening: Completely separate from quarterly SFSO data tracking, the
Board recommends that the SFSO promptly convene the Crowding Committee. The Committee
must meet at least annually, with consideration for twice-yearly meetings, regardless of whether
numerical triggers have been reached. This supports preventive planning without resolving the
disputed interpretation of existing policy. The Committee should assess whether funding, staffing,
technology, or programming limits alternatives to incarceration (such as electronic monitoring)
and if those limitations drive additional detention. The Committee must maintain written minutes,
attendance records, and documented corrective actions.

Ultimately, effective population management requires structural coordination among the SFSO, the
Judiciary, prosecutors, law enforcement, parole authorities, and health and community service providers.
Grand Jury Finding F13
Incidents of below minimum staffing result in facility lockdowns or lesser mobility restrictions, cause
suspended programming, reduce out-of-cell time, interrupt access to health services, impede visitation,
and have other negative impacts on inmates, yet data on lockdowns is unavailable.
SDOB Response to Finding Number Thirteen(F13)
The Sheriffs Department Oversight Board agrees with Finding F13 in its entirety and submits the
following written response:
Below-minimum staffing can result in facility lockdowns or lesser mobility restrictions, suspended
programming, reduced out-of-cell time, interrupted health services, impeded visitations, and other
negative impacts on people in custody. The absence of available lockdown data limits accountability and
the ability to evaluate and address these impacts. The Board encourages the San Francisco Sheriff's
Office (SFSO) to continue carefully tailoring lockdowns to the specific circumstances requiring them and
to avoid lockdowns whenever possible. [1]
The Board recognizes that overcrowding and understaffing affect people in custody, their families, and
staff. The correctional and justice system should coordinate to ensure that detention decisions remain
grounded in applicable legal standards and individualized circumstances while addressing crowding and
staffing pressures.
Importantly, neither overcrowding nor understaffing should impede timely, confidential access to
counsel—including in-person attorney visits—nor access to language services for incarcerated individuals
with Limited English Proficiency (LEP).

Page 3 of $
SDOB Response to Civil Grand Jury September 28, 2026
To ensure full operational compliance with the San Francisco Language Access Ordinance, the Board
recommends that the SFSO assess and report on how staffing shortages, lockdowns, and mobility
restrictions affect meaningful language access. This must include an evaluation of an individual’s ability
to communicate with counsel, request medical care, and obtain other essential services. The assessment
should specifically address access to qualified, court-certified interpreters (rather than a standard
telephone language line), bilingual staff, telephone interpretation, and translated booking and medical
materials.
Although language access was not specifically examined in the cited Civil Grand Jury report, Board
members raised serious concerns about unverified operational gaps. This includes an incident where an
incarcerated person was required to interpret for another individual regarding legal rights and court
attendance admonitions. The Board recommends reviewing these concerns and establishing strict
safeguards to ensure all timely, confidential communication occurs exclusively through qualified
language assistance.
The Board acknowledges members' reports of prior improvements in family notification and more
narrowly tailored lockdowns following engagement with the SFSO and the Inspector General. These
efforts should continue, including timely notifications of visitation disruptions and the protection of
essential services.
The Board recommends incorporating questions about lockdowns, mobility restrictions, and language
assistance into voluntary surveys of incarcerated individuals. These questions should allow individuals to
describe their experiences, including effects on medical care, programming, out-of-cell time, family
contact, and access to counsel. Participation must be voluntary, with appropriate confidentiality and
safeguards against retaliation. Survey responses should supplement operational records and identify
concerns for follow-up, rather than serving as standing, conclusive findings alone. This feedback process
ensures that incarcerated people can be heard even when administrative data are incomplete or
unavailable.
The Board further recommends standardized tracking, a public dashboard with appropriate confidentiality
protections, and regular reporting on lockdowns and mobility restrictions. The specific data requirements,
implementation measures, and reporting recommendations should be addressed separately in the Board's
response to R13A and R13B.

 5   While the Board of Supervisors acknowledges the report's finding that the jail occupancy rate

 6   exceeded 80% for over a year in 2025, and is on track to do so again in 2026, the threshold

 7   cited in the report is not the threshold for convening the Crowding Committee, but for

 8   conducting a Facility Needs Assessment (SFSO Policy 511.3). SFSO Policy 511.2 stipulates

 9   that the Sheriff convene a crowding committee when the number of incarcerated persons

10   exceeds the approved bed capacity of a facility, while SFSO Policy 511.3 requires the

11   department to initiate a jail needs assessment "in the event that each county jail maintains an

12   average 80 percent occupancy rate consistently for one year." The threshold for Policy 511.3

13   has been met, while the threshold for policy 511.2 has not yet been met. The Board of

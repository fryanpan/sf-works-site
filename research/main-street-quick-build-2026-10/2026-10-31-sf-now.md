## 2. The law

- **The quick-build definition** is in the 2019 SFMTA Board action, as SFMTA summarized it: "Improvements are reversible/adjustable", "Paint, posts and signs", "Meters and curb markings", "Traffic signal timing", "Transit boarding islands", and "Limited in duration to 24 months" (slide 7). The Board action was to "Define quick-build projects", "Modify Transportation Code to broaden City Traffic Engineer approval authority", "Create clear accountability and transparency" and "Approve locations for next set of potential quick-build projects" (slide 6). "Public Hearing required prior to parking and traffic modifications approval" (slide 9). [primary — read 2026-10-31] The code text itself was not opened; American Legal returned 403 to the road-diets lane the same day [unretrieved — verify].
- **SFMTA's current wording** of the same definition: "Quick-build projects are reversible, adjustable traffic safety improvements that can be installed relatively quickly ... constructed within weeks or months and are intended to be evaluated and reviewed within the initial 24 months of construction" ([sfmta.com/quickbuild](https://www.sfmta.com/quickbuild)) [primary — read 2026-10-31].
- **Board of Supervisors review, Transportation Code Division I §10.1** (Ordinance 127-18): a pending request stops implementation (§10.1(c)(4)), but decisions tied to implementing "a Bicycle Lane" or similar prior decisions are excluded from §10.1(a). Details and the amendment gap are in the road-diets note §2 [primary — read 2026-10-31, per repo note].
- **Street Safety Act, Resolution 437-25** (file 250851, adopted 10 to 0 on 2025-09-16, effective without the Mayor's signature). SFMTA §1(a): "Develop a plan for redesigning streets identified on the 2025 High Injury Network to enhance safety with tools such as providing separation … by December 2026". Public Works §4(a): "Design and build all recommended safety infrastructure improvements … when repaving ... on all designated High-Injury Network and arterial streets". SFCTA §8(a): "host an annual hearing on the review of street safety progress". The resolution "urges"; it does not bind. ([Legistar 250851](https://sfgov.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=7501574&GUID=965EDB61-B260-4A6E-A206-99CFF40B0266&FullText=1)) [Legistar — read 2026-10-07, per repo note] So **December 2026 is a dated hook**: SFMTA's HIN redesign plan is due then, and it is the natural place to ask for a mileage number [inference — mine].
- **Executive Directive 25-06**, Action 6: "Identify a focused list of priority Quick-Build projects that incorporate proven safety tools such as physical barriers, turn safety treatments, and other roadway safety features in areas where data shows they are necessary. (SFMTA)". Action 16 makes repaving upgrades "implement as funding permits". The directive sets no numeric target ([sf.gov release](https://www.sf.gov/news-mayor-lurie-signs-street-safety-executive-directive-in-major-step-to-improve-public-safety-in-san-francisco); directive read by OCR) [primary — read 2026-10-07, per repo note].
- **Signal timing for 20 to 25 mph** needs no state approval off state routes, as far as any document read says; the posted speed limit does (Vehicle Code §22352 and §22358.7, AB 43) [secondary — repo note, verify]. Not opened here.
- **No law requires a yearly mileage.** Every number in §3 is a plan target. [inference — mine, from the documents in §3]

- 85 miles over 2014 to 2023 is about 9.4 miles a year, but it counts work "in planning or construction" as well as finished work, so the finished pace is lower [inference — mine]. The 2021-to-2023 gain is about 2.5 miles a year [inference — mine].

- The 2019 program's first two lists: ten corridors, 7.5 centerline miles, then five more "Adopted by SFMTA Board on 6/4", 3.2 miles (2019 slides 11 and 12) [primary — read 2026-10-31]. 10.7 miles in total [inference — mine, sum].
- SFMTA's Quick-Build page lists 46 "Quick-Build Corridors Completed", 3 "Under Construction" (Beach Street, Oak Street/JFK Drive, Winston Drive) and 7 "Coming Soon" (Bosworth/Monterey, Brotherhood/Alemany, Cesar Chavez, Clarendon, Innes, Jefferson, Sutter) ([sfmta.com/quickbuild](https://www.sfmta.com/quickbuild)) [primary — read 2026-10-31]. The page gives no miles. Sutter Street is listed as coming soon although SFMTA's Early 2025 update says it was paused (road-diets note §8).

**SFMTA treats the HIN as two jobs**:
- The **Quick-Build Toolkit Project**: "every street intersection on the Vision Zero High Injury Network will receive core treatments, including crosswalk upgrades, daylighting, advanced limit lines, pedestrian head starts, and longer walk times" ([sfmta.com/quickbuild](https://www.sfmta.com/quickbuild)) [primary — read 2026-10-31].
- **Corridor projects**, the bundle this doc is about. The 2023 study: the core toolkit "can primarily be installed via SFMTA Shops with potentially few design, outreach, or legislative constraints" (p. 7), while road diets, sidewalk extensions, lower speed limits and protected bike lane upgrades "require additional resources, such as a planning process, design, outreach, and legislative constraints" (p. 8) [primary — read 2026-10-31].
- Of the 192 segments analyzed, the study recommends a road diet on 56 full corridors (29%), bicycle safety work on 99 (52%), the pedestrian toolkit (which includes bulb-outs, turn restrictions, protected left turns and signal retiming) on 111 (58%), and transit lanes or stop upgrades on 49 (26%) (Tables 5 and 6, PDF pp. 21 to 22) [primary — read 2026-10-31]. So the full bundle the ask names fits about a third to a half of the last 50 miles, by SFMTA's own screen [inference — mine].

## 4. Why about 9 miles a year: what SF's own documents say

No SFMTA document opened names the binding limit. What they do say:
- **Crews.** The 2019 program asked for "Additional resources to increase project delivery capacity" (slide 3): Paint Shop crews $2.1M to $2.5M, Sign Shop crews $1.1M to $1.4M, engineers, electricians and CAD technicians $0.9M to $1.3M, planning and outreach $0.3M to $0.5M, "Annual cost $4.4-5.7M", plus $2.7M one-time for trucks, materials and consultants (slide 13) [primary — read 2026-10-31]. So in 2019 SFMTA described the limit as shop and engineering staff, paid for by sales tax.
- **Money.** Two years later the Action Strategy still carried "approximately $5M annually in unfunded needs" for the whole-HIN goal (p. 32) [primary — read 2026-10-31]. Whether the 2022 capital plan funded it was not checked [unretrieved — verify]. The 2026-28 two-year capital budget carries "Citywide Vision Zero Quick-Build $14.7 million" (budget note) [primary — read 2026-10-20, per repo note], about $7.4M a year [inference — mine].
- **Process, on corridor work.** The 2023 study's "planning process, design, outreach, and legislative constraints" (p. 8) [primary — read 2026-10-31].
- **Street width, loading and fire access.** The Sutter Street pause: "The limited street width creates technical challenges in balancing the need for loading zones, parking, and emergency access while also improving safety" (road-diets note §8) [primary — read 2026-10-07, per repo note]. The SFFD manual has no maximum review period and puts Class IV bikeways in a one-month tier (paper trail note).
- **Appeals.** A §10.1 request stops implementation while pending; the road-diets note found one CEQA appeal of a lane removal (13th Street, 2017), heard and filed.
- **Political direction.** ED 25-06: "Safer streets are not anti-driving streets" and interventions should "not limit how or where people can travel" (p. 2), with "No new money or positions" (paper trail note) [primary — read 2026-10-07, per repo note]. A directive that frames the work this way gives staff no cover for lane removal [inference — mine].

So the record supports "crews and money" for the toolkit, and "process, outreach and political direction" for corridor redesigns, but it is SFMTA's description, not a measured breakdown [inference — mine]. The question to put to SFMTA in writing: staff hours, shop hours and calendar months per corridor mile, by phase, for the last 20 corridors.

- **Deaths and severe injuries:** no measurable change on SF's own quick-build corridors. The range runs from about a quarter fewer to about a quarter more, so a 15% to 30% drop like NYC's can be neither confirmed nor ruled out with these counts. [inference — mine]
- **Displacement:** counting two blocks each way does not make the walking result vanish (−12%), so the drop is not simply pushed to the next block. The data dive also found the corridors read about the same against the surrounding three to eight blocks (−10%, −20% to 0%). [DataSF nwes-mmgh — queried 2026-10-20, per repo note]
- **Regression to the mean:** the one-window-earlier test shows no drop before the work (+5% for walking with two blocks), which is evidence against "picked after a bad year". [inference — mine]
- **Not yet clear:** with 14 rows tested in the data dive, the walking row (p 0.018) falls just short after a Holm correction.
- **Corridor by corridor**, people hurt with two blocks, against the city: Taylor −16%, Howard −26%, Townsend −23%, California −29% fell; South Van Ness +21% and Evans +29% rose; Folsom (2nd to 5th, 2018) +3% (−19% to +32%) (injury data dive) [DataSF nwes-mmgh — queried 2026-10-20, per repo note]. The bundle varies by corridor, so these are not one design.
- **SFMTA's own count** across 27 of 34 projects, before and after with no comparison streets: all collisions "-2%", pedestrian "-32%", bike "-15%"; the 2023 report had −16%, −35% and −25% ([Safe Streets Evaluation 2024](https://www.sfmta.com/reports/safe-streets-evaluation-program-2024-annual-report)) [primary — read 2026-10-19, per repo note]. SFMTA's −32% for pedestrians and our −14% at the corridor count different things: SFMTA compares each street with itself, we compare it with the city's change over the same years. [inference — mine]
- **Speeds:** "the 6th Street Pedestrian Safety Quick-Build resulted in a 21% decrease in 85th percentile speeds" (Action Strategy, p. 27) [primary — read 2026-10-31], with no counts or dates given. California Street's 85th percentile stayed at 28 to 30 mph (road-diets note §4). No other SF speed result was found.
- **Muni:** California Street inbound AM median travel time +6%, outbound PM +12% (road-diets note §4). No other corridor read.

**For the reader:** the honest SF number today is "people hurt walking down about one in eight near the corridors, deaths and serious injuries not yet measurably changed". The advocate cannot yet tell a supervisor SF's quick-builds cut severe injuries by a given percent. [inference — mine]

## 6. Cost and money

From the 2023 study, for the last 50 HIN miles [primary — read 2026-10-31]:
- Core toolkit at intersections: "$4,038,000-$5,760,000" (p. 16); Table 4 subtotal $4,037,656 to $5,760,130 (PDF p. 20). Unit costs: daylighting $1,072.40 to $2,604.80 an intersection, continental crosswalk $4,000 to $6,000, leading pedestrian interval or slower walk speed $5,000.
- All intersection items with signal upgrades: $14,221,533 to $330,929,007 (Table 4). The top end is driven by accessible pedestrian signals at up to "$1,000,000.00" an intersection "if signal hardware is deficient".
- Corridor projects: "The cost for the corridor projects is estimated to be $46,613,000" (§5.2.1, PDF p. 22), about $932,000 a mile over 50 miles [inference — mine].
- Table 7 per mile: $400,000 (one corridor treatment), $709,500 (two), $1,707,825 (three or more); "EPC" means Equity Priority Community.
- **Table 7's totals leave out its own contingency column.** Low: $110,000 + $290,000 = $400,000, with the 10% contingency not added. High: $385,500 + $1,264,500 + $57,825 = $1,707,825, with the 20% not added. Medium: $330,000 + $330,000 + $49,500 = $709,500 in both columns, so the non-EPC figure carries the EPC mark-up and no 15% contingency. With contingency the per-mile figures would be about 10% to 20% higher. [inference — mine, arithmetic on Table 7]

- **Where SFMTA capital comes from:** "Only 15 percent of the CIP ($379 million) is city money"; the rest is grants. The Charter's Population Baseline transfer splits "75 percent to Muni reliability and repair and 25 percent to street safety" (budget note) [primary — read 2026-10-20, per repo note]. SFMTA's operating budget assumes November 2026 ballot revenue and a $200M state loan (budget note). So the money question touches Muni directly: street safety and Muni draw on the same baseline and the same agency budget [inference — mine]. What would be cut was not found [unretrieved — verify].
- **Upkeep:** no SFMTA figure for post and paint replacement was found [unretrieved — verify]. The 2019 crew request included "preventive maint." crews in the Sign Shop line (slide 13) [primary — read 2026-10-31].
- **Prop L** puts $187M over 30 years into "Safer and Complete Streets" (budget note) [primary — read 2026-10-20, per repo note]; how much of it is programmed to quick-builds was not read [unretrieved — verify].

The deaths reproduce (32 against "approximately 30"). The severe injuries do not: police reports give about 267 a year against "over 500". **The test** [inference — mine]:
1. Extend the 31-corridor test to every corridor on the Quick-Build page (46 completed), with install dates from project pages, and report killed or severely injured and kids 5 to 18 as their own rows. Pool by bundle: corridors with a lane removed, with protected bike lanes, toolkit only.
2. Matched comparison: each treated segment against untreated main-street segments of the same street class, HIN status, lane count (from OSM, with its known lag) and pre-period injury count, staggered difference in differences, with the one-window-earlier placebo and the two-block displacement ring the data dive already uses.
3. Pace: rebuild "treated HIN miles by year" from the corridor list and centerlines, against the 13-a-year and 20-a-year targets, so the doc can show the delivered pace instead of SFMTA's "completed or in planning" count.
4. School-first against HIN-first: rank the rebuilt last-50 pieces and the 130 main-street miles two ways (killed or severely injured per mile; kids hurt within 660 feet of a school per mile, as `tools/school-trips/school-calm-priority.py` does), and report the injury-weighted miles each order reaches in years 1 to 5, split by Equity Priority Community.
5. Measures to hold SFMTA to, yearly: treated miles finished (not "in planning"), police-reported and hospital-linked killed or severely injured on treated corridors against untreated main streets, 85th percentile and share over 25 and 30 mph at the same counters, Muni run time on the corridor.

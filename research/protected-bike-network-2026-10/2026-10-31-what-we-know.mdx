# Protected Bike Network: What We Know

The change: SF builds its all ages and abilities bikeways (the [Biking and Rolling Plan](https://www.sfmta.com/media/41810/download)'s term for paths, protected lanes, calm shared streets and comfortable painted lanes) as one connected network within a quarter mile of every resident, on a dated schedule, instead of as separate corridor projects. The plan calls that full network the "North Star".

**In short:**

- **Does it work?** Two crash-site studies found injury odds on curb- or barrier-separated lanes about 90% lower than on major streets with no lane, but each rests on very few injuries, so the true cut could be 46% to 98% in one and 5% to 99% in the other. Posts and paint, mostly what quick-build is, did no better than no lane in one study and halved crashes against paint in a federal study that includes SF. US cities with more protected lanes have fewer deaths and serious injuries for everyone, a link that doesn't prove cause. Whether a network beats the same miles built as corridors is untested in the US.
- **Which way works best?** No study compares two ways of building on the same outcome [inference — mine]. Calming neighbourhood streets with traffic filters has the strongest controlled safety result (London), and pop-up lanes the fastest ridership gain (Europe, 2020). In SF, quick-build costs about what SFMTA budgets for bike and corridor projects over four years, so it means delaying other projects rather than finding new money; concrete needs new money; and the plan says current staff can't grow to build either at network pace.
- **How would we know?** Each quarter, report residents within a quarter mile by district, the share of all-ages miles in the largest connected stretch, monthly bike counts, and bicyclists killed or severely injured. SF publishes only the last as data.

## Cost, Benefit and Support

**Pricing the 154 decided miles at quick-build rates gives about $113M ($71M to $243M), with 21 miles not yet priced.** Over a 15-year life it breaks even on bicyclist injuries alone if posts and paint cut them by about 7%, a cut no US study has measured for a network, and the crash-site studies found light separation no safer than no lane. The curbed version, which those studies do support, would need a 31% to 41% cut and returns 0.2 to 0.6 on our assumptions. Prices are budgets at approval.

Unit prices are five SFMTA quick-build budgets ($0.89M to $2.39M a street mile) and Central Embarcadero for concrete ($16M to $24M), from `data/school-budget/unit-costs.json` [secondary — repo, verify].

At a 10% cut over 15 years the benefit-cost ratio is 1.5, and 0.2 to 5.7 across our low and high cases; above 1, benefits exceed costs. The assumed cut is a judgement, since no study measures a network effect in a US city; the ratio uses a 3.1% discount rate and leaves out pedestrians, drivers, health from riding, lost parking and delay [inference — mine]. **An opponent's strongest point:** on injuries alone, the central case costs about $2.2M per person killed or severely injured avoided over 10 years, against $0.13M to $1.1M for redesigning High Injury Network streets with lower limits and $0.84M for speed humps, though those count every road user [secondary — sibling repo notes, verify]. The network's case is the riding it opens to children, older people and people without cars, which no SF number values yet.

## How to Tell Whether It Helped

**SF can measure coverage now but not riding, and nobody has tested connection against the same miles of corridors.**

Miles built and coverage show only that the program happened. The strongest tests are scoring SF's own links by connection at staggered dates, and a lottery for build order within a priority tier (evaluation note, methods 7 and 8) [inference — mine].

Harm checks: car traffic on parallel streets (Ghent's ring road rose 14% at peaks and one inner road 40%; on South Van Ness's road diet, injuries within two blocks rose 21% against the city); Muni stop-to-stop times (on California Street's road diet, inbound morning buses took 6% longer and outbound afternoon buses 12%) [secondary — repo notes, verify]; fire response times from SFFD's dispatch records ([nuek-vuh3](https://data.sfgov.org/d/nuek-vuh3)); and injuries at intersections and the network's edges.

## What This Means

The next decision is the SFMTA Board's: the High Injury Network redesign plan it owes by December 2026, then its next capital budget. That plan is where to ask for a dated crosstown grid, a ranked gap list, and quarterly reporting of residents within a quarter mile by district and the share of all-ages miles in the largest connected stretch. The Supervisors' levers are Prop L money and the budget veto; the state's are grant rules and Caltrans streets. Recheck after the 2026-11-03 vote on Prop G and the Charter measures.

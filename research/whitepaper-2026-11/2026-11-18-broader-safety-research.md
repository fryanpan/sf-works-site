## 1. Dutch policy on speed, volume and mass

Source: SWOV, *Sustainable Safety 3rd edition: The advanced vision for 2018-2030*, [SWOV](https://www.swov.nl/node/17085/). Page numbers are the report's own. [primary — read 2026-11-18]

- **Mass has been a named principle since 1992.** Table 3 (p. 13) traces the principle from "Homogeneity in mass, speed and direction" (1992-2010 and 2005-2020 editions) to "(Bio)mechanics: minimizing differences in speed, direction, mass and size whilst maximizing protection of the road user" (2018-2030).
- **Separation is the rule for any big difference, not only speed.** Fast traffic "is separated, either physically or in time, from slow moving traffic, from traffic travelling in opposite direction, from traffic with a substantially different mass or width, from hazardous obstacles, and from vulnerable road users" (p. 18).
- **A mode that can't meet the criteria is kept off through streets.** "If a mode of transport cannot meet the speed, mass, size and road user protection criteria that are necessary for a safe outcome, then this mode will not be allowed on roads intended for a flow function", and gets "special infrastructure ... adapted to traffic with low speed and small size and mass" (p. 18).
- **Volume enters through road function.** Flow and exchange "do not combine safely and are best kept separate" (p. 15), and dwelling uses such as playing and shopping "do not combine safely with traffic, least of all with through-traffic" (p. 15). A street may mix the two only "when through-going cyclists travel at a safe speed, there is limited local access traffic, and the speed differences are small", and SWOV calls that "not ideal" (p. 16).
- **Planners own traffic volume.** "Spatial planners plan community and neighbourhood development patterns that lead to a safe hierarchical road network structure, thus influencing travel distances, routes, road transport modes, and traffic volumes" (p. 26).
- **Where separation isn't there yet, everyone slows to the most vulnerable user.** "The speed of all traffic should be adapted ... to the most vulnerable transport modes (in particular, pedestrians and cyclists) and road users (in particular, children and seniors)" (p. 19). On 50 km/h roads cars and bikes "should be physically separated because of the large differences in speed", yet only about 60% had a separate cycle track (p. 6).
- **SWOV names growing vehicle mass as an open problem.** "Traffic is getting increasingly more heterogenic because of new transport modes and a greater variety in mass and size of existing transport modes", and it asks whether vehicle makers or road authorities should answer for it (p. 19). Drivers face higher task demands "when the vehicle constitutes a greater danger to others (e.g. due to its greater mass or speed)" (p. 22).
- **SUVs.** SWOV's fact sheet *How safe are SUVs?* says SUVs are safe for their occupants but carry more risk for other traffic. ([SWOV fact sheet](https://swov.nl/en/fact/safe-passenger-cars-7-how-safe-are-suvs)) [web search — verify]: the page sits behind a bot check and was read only as a search snippet.
- **Gap.** The volume thresholds Dutch designers use to choose mixed traffic, bike lanes or separate tracks are in CROW's *Design Manual for Bicycle Traffic* (2016), which is sold, not published. [unretrieved — verify]

**What SF could do differently** [inference — mine]: classify every street by one job (through, distributor, access) and set its speed and separation from that; on access streets, take out through traffic, not only lower the limit; and treat vehicle mass as a design input, with trucks and large vehicles routed to through streets.

## 2. Seoul's safety framework

No English source we found calls Seoul's framework clear, so which one Bryan means is a guess. The most likely is **Safe Speed 5030** inside Seoul's 3rd Traffic Safety Basic Plan: one rule maps street type to speed, and the plan names one priority and one number. [inference — mine] Candidates:

- **Seoul 3rd Traffic Safety Basic Plan, 2017-2021 (most likely).** Seoul set out to cut traffic deaths from 376 (2015) to 180 by 2021 and pedestrian deaths from 213 to under 63, and to lower arterial limits "60km/h→50km/h" and local streets ("이면도로 등 생활권도로") to 30 km/h. Its stated priority: "보행자사망자 70% 감축을 목표로 보행자 안전대책을 최우선으로 추진한다" ("aiming at a 70% cut in pedestrian deaths, pedestrian safety measures come first"). ([Seoul Metropolitan Government, 2017-04-11](https://news.seoul.go.kr/traffic/archives/32983)) [primary — read 2026-11-18]
- **Result.** Seoul's own series: 376 deaths in 2015, 243 in 2021, 180 in 2023, 212 in 2024; pedestrian deaths 213, 124, 90 and 117 in the same years. The 2021 targets were missed. ([Seoul traffic accident statistics, updated 2026-04-07](https://news.seoul.go.kr/traffic/archives/335)) [primary — read 2026-11-18 by a research subagent]
- **National Safe Speed 5030, from 2021-04-17.** After 100 days, the police, Ministry of Land and TS Korea reported pedestrian deaths in covered areas down 16.7%, "2.7 times greater than in non-applied areas" ([Asia Business Daily, 2021-08-11](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2021081110332891938)) [secondary — verify]. A short year-on-year window, reported by the agencies themselves. In 2022, 35 sections went back to 60 km/h, 17 of them on Seoul's Han River bridges, as "sections with less correlation to pedestrian safety" ([Asia Business Daily, 2022-10-24](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2022102420043038213)) [secondary — verify].
- **Pedestrian-priority roads and stop-for-pedestrians rule, from 2022-07-12.** Pedestrians may use the whole roadway, with a speed cap as low as 20 km/h; drivers must stop for a person "about to cross". Seoul named 115 such roads, but "enforcement is virtually nonexistent" ([Asia Business Daily, 2024-09-03](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2024090216554521906)) [secondary — verify].
- **Pedestrian Special City, 2nd plan, 2019-2023** ("걷는 도시, 서울"): ₩642bn, 53 projects, walking from 16.7% to 20% of trips and pedestrian deaths per 100,000 from 1.67 to 0.84 ([Seoul](https://news.seoul.go.kr/traffic/archives/501602)) [primary — read 2026-11-18 by a research subagent]. No results published on the page.
- **Gaps.** Seoul's 4th plan (2022-2026), the ITF Korea road safety profile and the 2020 school-zone law were not opened. [unretrieved — verify]

**What SF could do differently** [inference — mine]: write one speed per street class into city policy (about 30 mph on arterials, 20 mph everywhere else, the 5030 rule in miles); publish a pedestrian-death target with a path for each year and the actual count beside it; and require any exception to the speed rule to show it doesn't touch pedestrian safety, listed street by street.

Note: Bryan's guidelines under this heading (separate modes, measure stress) read closer to Dutch Sustainable Safety than to anything Seoul published; he may be blending the two. [inference — mine]

## 3. What SFPD crash reports and DPH injury records hold today

Read 2026-11-18, partly by a research subagent; downloads in `scratch/runs/crash-data-fields-2026-11-18/`. DataSF now serves from data.sf.gov.

| Ask | Status | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vehicle year | Collected and published | `vehicle_year` in the parties table [8gtc-pjc6](https://data.sf.gov/d/8gtc-pjc6); 101,954 of 139,619 party rows filled, 4,007 of 4,383 drivers in 2024 [DataSF 8gtc-pjc6 — queried 2026-11-18] |
| Vehicle make | Collected and published | `vehicle_make`, 125,364 rows filled (12,006 of them "-") [DataSF 8gtc-pjc6 — queried 2026-11-18] |
| Vehicle model, VIN | Collected, not published | CHP 555 item 41 asks for "manufacturer trade name ... model series name, and color, for example, Chev/Camaro/Whi"; item 46 VIN "when a license plate is not available" ([CHP Collision Investigation Manual ch. 3, rev. April 2021](https://www.chp.ca.gov/siteassets/policy/hpm/hpm-110.5/hpm-110.5-ch-3.pdf)) [primary — read 2026-11-18]. No model field in DataSF or in SWITRS ([TIMS codebook](https://tims.berkeley.edu/help/crashdata/switrs-codebook.php)) [primary — read 2026-11-18] |
| Non-injury (property damage) crashes | Mostly not collected | DGO 9.02: "Members need not investigate or report non-injury (property damage) vehicle crashes" except hit-and-run, city vehicles, DUI, arrests, school buses and all autonomous-vehicle crashes ([SFPD DGO 9.02](https://sf.gov/sites/default/files/2023-07/Department%20General%20Order%209.02%20Vehicle%20Crashes%20CLEAN%20%2820230517%29.pdf)) [primary — read 2026-11-18]. No DataSF crash table holds them; SFPD incident reports [wg3w-h783](https://data.sf.gov/d/wg3w-h783) carry only "Traffic Collision, Hit & Run, Property Damage", 1,097 since 2018 [DataSF — queried 2026-11-18]. The near-universal record is the driver's SR-1 to the DMV for damage over $1,000 (VC §16000) [secondary — verify] |
| Injuries with no police report | Collected, not published at record level | DPH links ZSFG trauma records to police reports; "granular hospital data are not distributable to the public", and "~60% of records classified as severe appeared both in police and hospital data" ([SFDPH, Severe Injury Trends 2015-2022, Dec 2024](https://visionzerosf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Severe-Injury-Trends_2015-2022.pdf)) [primary — read 2026-11-18]. It misses people treated at Kaiser, Sutter and other systems (same report) |

- **Correction for the outline.** SFPD already records and publishes year and make; the ask is the model, or a VIN-decoded body class and weight. [inference — mine]
- **Not checked.** Whether DGO 9.02 is still the version in force; the HCAI statewide emergency-department data with external-cause codes, a possible source for crashes treated outside ZSFG. [unretrieved — verify]

**What SF could do differently** [inference — mine]: SFPD publishes the model already on the CHP 555, with a VIN decode to body class and curb weight, so the city can count injuries by vehicle size; SFPD takes property-damage reports online (the CHP 555-03 self-report already exists, per [CIM ch. 9](https://www.chp.ca.gov/siteassets/policy/hpm/hpm-110.5/hpm-110.5-ch-9.pdf)) and publishes them as a near-miss layer; DPH publishes the linked hospital records quarterly as anonymized, block-level rows, and the city adds Kaiser and Sutter to the linkage or uses HCAI data.

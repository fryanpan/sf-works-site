*Written public comment sent to the San Francisco County Transportation Authority Board for its [17 November 2026 meeting](https://www.sfcta.org/events/transportation-authority-board-143), item 8 (Street Safety Initiative update).*

---

Dear Chair Melgar and Commissioners,

Thank you for the update on item 8.  One year into the Street Safety Initiative, the city continues to implement changes, but also we continue to make almost no progress on city-wide outcomes.  

The main reason for this is that we continue ignoring one of the main recommendations from the 2025 Civil Grand Jury. In their "Failed Vision" report they asked SFMTA to focus on "area-based" work instead of single intersections or a few street blocks at a time.  The mayor says SFMTA already works this way, and I've investigated and seen that they largely aren't able to plan or execute in an area-based way. 

This email has more details, and I'll publish a whitepaper shortly with a more in-depth investigation. 

Instead of what we've done so far, I would like us to focus on the highest priority communities as a whole (neighborhoods and schools).  I want us to understand the transportation needs of these entire communities.  And then mainly use quick, cost-effective "area-based" solutions to deliver community-wide outcomes within a year -- slower speeds, less conflict with traffic, and more safe and convenient routes to places people want to go.

Thanks,  
Bryan Chan

## Measure Success by Community.  Not By Intersection.

Today, the city often measures success by number of interventions installed, and how well they work on that street block.  In my review of public injury data, our city's 33 camera systems are likely reducing injuries at the location where they're installed.  That's great, and we deserve to celebrate!  However, when I looked 2 blocks up or down the street, our cameras have made no significant difference.  [WalkSF saw similar results](https://walksf.org/2026/10/28/what-do-drivers-do-after-theyre-past-a-speed-camera/) when they used a speed gun a few blocks from one camera site.

There are about 2,000 blocks on the 2024 High Injury Network, and about 250 schools.  Plus thousands of blocks not on the HIN where people still feel unsafe.  When you implement changes that affect one block at a time, each local fix barely makes a dent on the city overall.

You're bringing a squirt gun to a balloon fight.  And it's frankly not working.

The citywide car speed slowdown since speed cameras came into effect is an interesting result.  SFMTA noted it's likely due to city-wide advertising causing that effect rather than the speed camera locations themselves.  If that's true, then that's the kind of area-based effect we're looking for!

### How to Measure a Community

Measure success across whole communities by gathering regular data across most of the roads in a community about the following:

- Speeding
- Red light violations
- Turn violations
- Traffic stress levels
- Pedestrian, bicycle, and transit volume and mode share

Devices like the Telraam work well for some of these metrics.  While Telraam doesn't give perfect absolute counts compared to SFMTA's wires, they're cheaper and easier to deploy.  Plus directionally they're good enough to make the right decisions at a community level.

I would also recommend measuring how residents feel about safety.  When I surveyed 94 San Franciscans using Pollfish this week, these were the aspects of safety they cared about most:

- Safety from crime during trips
- Safety from traffic stress during trips(including cars speeding through residential neighborhoods, cutting off pedestrians, near misses, etc.)

This is consistent with multiple city-wide surveys and CBTP surveys.

Residents have a broader definition of what "safety" means.  And sadly, in my survey, I also saw that 61 of 94 residents said the city listens to people like them only a little or not at all.

If you truly care about safety, then measure whole communities and measure what they care about.

## Use Tools That Work Across A Community

Setting clear goals is the first challenge.  Executing is the second part -- the majority of SFMTA's safety budget has gone into work on corridors or individual intersections using tools like speed humps that affect one block.

We don't yet use the area-based tools that international best practice recommends.

Here are some examples of tools that work better:

### Use Queensland's Mobile Speed Camera Method for Broader, More Equitable Impact

Work with the state to show that mobile speed cameras, used carefully, actually help minimize surveillance and keep more people safe in equity priority neighborhoods while having less impact from ticket fines.

Here's how this could work:

- Identify 1000 locations that cover the whole HIN and all school zones for potential camera placement
- Rotate 100-200 mobile cameras randomly between all locations on a daily basis
- Change the random camera assignment each month based on equity and speeding data
- This gives you a much broader area-impact on speeding  
  With less surveillance overall and fewer fines for low-income residents 
- And gives you a way to measure speeds and speeding everywhere

### Implement Low Traffic Neighborhoods (LTN) Beside Corridor Interventions

Corridors like BRP slow streets or changes on HIN main streets often lead to negative side effects for residents next door, and then people complain.  If we built LTNs as well at the same time, that might help both amplify the effect of each corridor, and also give nearby residents benefits and bring more of them on board.

Here's how this could work:

- For each corridor project (e.g. BRP Slow Street, HIN corridor enhancements)  
  Also quick build a LTN beside it so the traffic and problems don't just move next door
- A few carefully chosen bollards (a few hundred thousand dollars) can block through traffic from going through an entire neighborhood
- And there's minimal impact on people who live there, or on fire response  
  e.g. in London, one study found LTNs didn't slow fire response

In a Pollfish survey I did this week of 94 San Franciscans, nearly twice as many wanted this as didn't (38 to 20), even if the LTN changes added 30 seconds longer to each drive out of their neighborhood.  Getting traffic out of side streets was worth it to them.

LTNs also create new nearly car-free community spaces.  About a quarter of the survey respondents (23 of 94) said they'd love to put a community garden or parklet into these spaces.  And 42 wanted to keep the space as parking. 

### Give Each Kid (and Their Parents) a Safe Connection to School

School (and workplaces, churches, or other communities) are one of the most essential places to improve safety.  In addition to the [Safe School Connections](https://www.sfmta.com/projects/safe-school-connections) efforts, which mainly focus on the block closest to school, I would like to see us use these effective area-based tools:

- Implement an LTN around the school
- Identify key Biking & Rolling Plan Slow Street segments that give the most kids & parents a safe path to school
  - Build these segments quickly with an LTN around them
- Implement random mobile speed cameras to enforce 15mph school zones
- Fund the e-bike incentive program, and launch trials in schools
- Build raised crosswalks at HIN main streets within 1/4 mile of school  
  Only 15 % of kids injuries during school hours happen on the last block  
  Another 7% within the rest of the school zone  
  And 39% between the school zone and a 1/4 mile away, mainly at main streets
- Implement School Streets at all schools within a year wherever possible  
  Crossing guards often already patrol either end of a street that works  
  They could put up temporary barriers & move them for residents/school busses

If you deploy multiple tools from the list above at a high priority school, that likely will be enough to show a community-wide impact on key outcomes.

This is just a brief overview of some tools with an area-based effect that are worth adding to the toolkit.

## Run Pilot Projects By Community

When the Civil Grand Jury asked for more "area-based projects," Mayor Lurie highlighted our CBTPs as an example that working on whole areas was already standard practice.

When I looked into how we run CBTPs and other projects, I realized how much more we could be doing.  Our CBTPs and other bigger visions like BRP do indeed plan out wider areas, but often come without the funding or execution plan to actually deliver measurable safety outcomes to the target community.  

Often these plans get implemented one small piece at a time, where each piece requires finding funding and fighting resistant residents to get anything done.

Can we try the following on more projects instead? (as SFCTA+SFMTA kind of did on the Bayview CBTP)

1. Promise a community a budget (e.g. $2M per neighborhood, $250k per school) and that you will roll out, on a pilot basis, the top changes the community prioritizes with SFMTA in 1 year or less.  With agreed on community-wide safety outcomes to hit.
2. Use deliberative budgeting and democracy techniques to engage the whole community in 1-2 months
   1. Focus on solutions that use area-based tools
   2. Use tools that fix one block at a time only on the worst blocks 
   3. Only use cheap/quick build tools that fit within budget
   4. Use AI-assisted deliberation  
      And AI-assisted paper + telephone interviews for those that don't have access to smartphone/computer
   5. Give community organizations these tools too to help them do their work
3. Roll out changes within 3-6 months
4. Get feedback after everyone can see and feel the changes and has a chance to adapt to them
   1. e.g. London waits 5 months after LTN deployment before measuring
5. If the community doesn't like the results, roll back the pieces that didn't work

## In Short

What we've done so far hasn't worked.  I hope and expect that we will actually make progress on addressing the safety needs of our residents.

I will be publishing a whitepaper shortly that will go into each topic above in more detail.  Plus I plan on giving residents and advocacy organizations more ways to understand existing public data, tools to do area-based planning, and ways for communities to deliberate and participate in coming up with solutions.

I will also happily work with anyone in the city to move these efforts forward, and I also plan to empower more residents and local advocacy organizations to do the same.

Best,  
Bryan Chan

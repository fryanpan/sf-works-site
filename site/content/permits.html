Where a permit waits

San Francisco publishes one figure per permit, and that figure combines three different things: time sitting in a queue, time actually under review, and time the applicant spends answering comments. Separating them changes what the number means and who it points at.

Waiting is not reviewing, and only one of them is the problem

Across every desk together, the median first review reaches a reviewer in {{ allQueueP50 }} days and then takes {{ allReviewP50 }} days to review. Even at the tail — the one review in ten that goes worst — it waits {{ allQueueP90 }} days and reviews in {{ allReviewP90 }}.

The queue is gone. The time is in review. That is the opposite of what the single published figure suggests, and it matters because the two have different fixes: a permit sitting in a queue needs routing, and a permit under review needs reviewers.

When the queue disappeared

A median of zero is what a data artifact usually looks like, so the next question is when it changed and whether the change is real. It is, and it happened in one year: {{ turnYear }}.

Through {{ tensLastYear }} the typical permit waited between {{ tensQueueMin }} and {{ tensQueueMax }} days before a reviewer opened it, and the slowest one in ten waited up to {{ tensQueueP90Months }} months. The wait then fell steadily, reaching {{ declineEndQueueP50 }} days by {{ declineEndYear }}, and in {{ turnYear }} it dropped to {{ turnQueueP50 }} day. It has been {{ lastQueueP50 }} since, with the slowest one in ten at {{ lastQueueP90 }} days.

Review time did not follow. It ran {{ tensReviewMin }} to {{ tensReviewMax }} days through {{ tensLastYear }}, rose as far as {{ declineReviewMax }} days in the years after, and has come back to {{ lastReviewP50 }} days — roughly where it started. Nothing in this series shows review getting faster. What changed is that the queue in front of it went away, which is why the review time is now the whole of the wait.

Every cohort in that table is measured the same way, which is the only reason it can be read. A filing-year series is right-censored by default: a permit still sitting in a queue has no completion date, drops out of the count, and makes the recent years look faster than they are. So each year counts only first reviews that finished within {{ cohortWindowDays }} days of filing — the 2015 rows on the same terms as the 2025 rows. The queue collapse survives that correction, which is what makes it a change rather than an artifact.

The same table carries its own corroboration in the last column: the share of filings finishing inside that window rose from {{ midKeptPct }}% in {{ midYear }} to {{ lastKeptPct }}% in {{ lastYear }}. More permits are finishing, and they are finishing sooner. A censoring artifact would show the opposite.

What the data does not say is why. It records dates, not decisions. The change lines up with the city's move to a new permitting system, but that is my reading, not something the dataset states.

Three things this table deliberately does not do. It counts only first reviews, because the filing date does not advance between rounds — computed across all rounds it reports a two-month queue everywhere, which is arithmetic and not a queue. It computes every span from the raw filing, arrival and completion dates rather than the city's published duration, which starts its clock at a different event depending on review type. And it drops desks with fewer than {{ minPerStation }} reviews, because below that a "one in ten" is just the single worst case.

What has teeth, and what does not

The {{ targetDays }}-day plan review figure and the {{ targetBizDays }}-day online target are both the city measuring itself. Missing them carries no consequence.

State law is different. California Government Code §65913.3, added by AB 2234 in 2022, sets deadlines in business days for post-entitlement permits on housing developments:

That is the only consequence anywhere in this comparison with any force behind it — and it applies to a much narrower set of permits than the ones counted on this page, so the figures above are not a measure of compliance with it.

Why this page sits beside housing

Permit timing and housing output are the same question at two ends of one pipeline: where housing stalls, and whether it arrives. A queue at a plan review desk and an affordable share that moved only because market-rate completions collapsed are two views of the same city.

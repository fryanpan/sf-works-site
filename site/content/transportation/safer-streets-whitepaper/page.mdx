## Part 1: A Failed Safety Process <span class="coming">Coming soon</span>

On street safety, we’ve failed to do what people need.  City efforts to address safety challenges, including Vision Zero adopted in 2014 and the Street Safety Initiative in 2025, have both failed to bring down the number of people severely injured on our streets compared to 2014.  

Why have we failed? Here are the main problems we look into:

1. **People care about a broader definition of safety.** Serious injuries and deaths are a major problem, but people have also regularly said in surveys that they feel unsafe due to traffic stress and crime.
2. **The High Injury Network (HIN) leads to poor decision-making.** Focusing on street segments dehumanizes us. We lose details about how people crashed into each other, when, and why.  Plus, where were they coming from and going to?  This leads us to make poor decisions.
3. **We prioritize using spot fixes to solve city-wide problems.** Motivated by the HIN, most of the traffic calming we approve changes one intersection or a few blocks along a corridor.  We don't prioritize more cost-effective, area-based fixes.

Each problem above narrows our focus further.  As a result, we're left with a vision that doesn't match what people want, and we make little to no progress on the vision we have.

Part 1 investigates each problem above in more detail and proposes alternative solutions based on international best practices.  Where public data allows, we also built prototypes of better tools (like an alternate Street Safety map to supplement the HIN).

## Part 2: A Better Alternative: Community-Wide Pilot Projects <span class="coming">Coming soon</span>

How might we do better?  Part 2 outlines an alternative strategy that builds on the Civil Grand Jury's 2025 report, [Failed Vision](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/2025_CGJ_Report_Failed_Vision.pdf#page=36). The Jury asked SFMTA to "adopt standard methods for evaluating area-based street safety", looking at "the overall street network, not just individual intersections or corridors" (Recommendation 3.2).

Our community-wide approach takes this one step further. By community, we mean any group of people in San Francisco that's cohesive enough for us to deploy multiple safety tools across the community and measure meaningful impact within a year.  A community can be, for example, a neighborhood, a school, or a workplace.

To do this, we build on tools from Part 1 and propose an alternative process:

1. **Measure data and outcomes in a whole community**
   1. Measure how the community feels about traffic safety
   2. Measure community-wide safety measures across a large sample of street blocks
2. **Use community-based tools for community problems**
   1. Prioritize tools that cost-effectively change a whole area for area-based problems
   2. Only use tools that change one block for the worst intersections and corridors
3. **Do pilots that show impact quickly across a community**
   1. Depend on tools (or community resources) that have the capacity to make changes quickly
   2. Then implement multiple tools across a whole community within months

We've had Community-Based Transportation Plans (CBTPs) before, and they have included some aspects of the approach above.  But usually, most of the budget in these plans has gone toward a laundry list of local spot fixes; we haven't defined or measured community-wide outcomes afterward, and we've failed to implement most items in each CBTP because of lack of confirmed budget or other constraints.
